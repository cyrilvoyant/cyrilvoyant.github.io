sCV and F: variability and forecastability of solar irradiance

Principle 2 · The model at the coreThe approach

In the story: understand before you compute. sCV and F say how variable and how forecastable a site is before any model is fitted.

In brief

  • sCV measures how much clouds make irradiance deviate from clear sky, scaled so that 0 is a clear sky and 1 a sky switching at random between clear sky and overcast.
  • F = 1 + sCV(ρmax − 1) adds the memory of the clouds at the forecast horizon, and predicts forecast difficulty better than sCV alone, on synthetic series and 68 stations of the Spanish SIAR network.
  • Published in Renewable Energy 256, 123913 (2026) by C. Voyant, A. Julien, M. Despotovic, G. Notton, L. A. Garcia-Gutierrez, C. F. Nicolosi, P. Blanc and J. Bright; MATLAB code, doi:10.5281/zenodo.23562553.

How variable is the sun, and how forecastable? sCV and F explained

Before choosing a forecasting model, it helps to know how difficult the site is. Two numbers answer that: sCV, the variability that clouds add to the course of the sun, and F, how much of that variability can be anticipated at a given horizon.

The theory

Before choosing a model, describe how difficult the site is: how much the clouds add to the course of the sun (sCV), and how much of it can be anticipated at a horizon (F).

Why the usual coefficient of variation misleads

\(\mathrm{CV}=\sigma/\mu\) mostly measures the day: the sun rises and sets, so a perfectly clear sky already has a large CV (about 1.2 in the figure below), and two sites with the same clouds but different day lengths get different CVs.

Definitions

\(I_t\)
measured global irradiance
\(I^{\mathrm{clr}}_t\)
clear-sky irradiance at the same time and place
\(\mu_{\mathrm{clr}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{clr}}\)
mean and standard deviation of \(I^{\mathrm{clr}}\) (night hours included)
\(d\)
diffuse floor: fraction of clear sky left under a thick cloud, \(d=0.2\)
\(\varepsilon_t=I_t-I^{\mathrm{clr}}_t\)
the part the sun's course does not explain
\(n\)
forecast horizon, in time steps; \(T\) steps per day

sCV: variability around clear sky

$$\mathrm{sCV}=\frac{\sqrt2}{|d-1|}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{RMSE}\!\left(I,I^{\mathrm{clr}}\right)}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{clr}}^2+\mu_{\mathrm{clr}}^2}},$$ $$\mathrm{RMSE}\!\left(I,I^{\mathrm{clr}}\right)=\sqrt{\tfrac1N\textstyle\sum_t\varepsilon_t^2}.$$

The numerator is the error of the clear-sky model used as a forecast; \(\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{clr}}^2+\mu_{\mathrm{clr}}^2}\) is the root mean square of the clear-sky curve, so night hours change nothing. The factor \(\sqrt2/|d-1|\) sets the reference value: sCV = 0 for a clear sky and sCV = 1 for a sky that switches at random, half of the time, between clear sky and the diffuse floor. 1 is a reference, not a maximum: a sky that stays at the diffuse floor (\(I=d\,I^{\mathrm{clr}}\)) gives \(\sqrt2\).

F: forecastability at a horizon

With \(\rho_\varepsilon(k)\) the autocorrelation of \(\varepsilon\) at lag \(k\) and \(\rho_{\max}=\max_{n\le k\le T/2}\rho_\varepsilon(k)\):

$$F=1+\mathrm{sCV}\,\left(\rho_{\max}-1\right).$$

For \(0\le\rho_{\max}\le1\), F lies between \(1-\mathrm{sCV}\) (clouds with no memory) and 1 (clouds that last). A cloudy site can be forecastable if its clouds persist. sCV describes the site; F describes the forecasting task at a horizon.

Assumptions and validity

A clear-sky model is needed. Use at least a year of data and a time step of a few minutes or more. The relation between F and forecast error was established on synthetic series and on 68 stations of the Spanish SIAR network (see the results below).

F predicts forecast error

This is the article's conclusion on synthetic series and on 68 stations of the Spanish SIAR network: F is the better predictor of how hard forecasting will be, sCV the better description of variability. Use at least a year of data and a time step of a few minutes or more.

Code

The repository gives scv.m and forecastability.m (base MATLAB), a demonstration that writes the figures above, and the article's own scripts, unchanged.
