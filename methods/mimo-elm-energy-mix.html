MIMO-ELM: forecasting every source of an island electricity grid at once

Principle 4 · Every tool, at the peripheryThe approach

In the story: when no equation links the sources, let the machine learn, simply, parsimoniously, frugally: one ELM forecasts seven grid signals at once (nRMSE 0.179 for solar, 0.051 for thermal, one hour ahead).

In brief

  • One extreme learning machine forecasts the seven signals of the Corsican electricity grid at once (total, thermal, imports, hydropower, solar, wind, bioenergy) from the last 48 hours.
  • One hour ahead, the published study reports nRMSE 0.179 for solar and 0.051 for thermal, with R² above 0.98, ahead of persistence and of an LSTM tuned with a realistic budget.
  • Published in Applied Energy 410, 127599 (2026) by C. Voyant, M. Despotovic, L. Garcia-Gutierrez, M. Asloune, Y.-M. Saint-Drenan, J.-L. Duchaud, G. A. Faggianelli and E. Magliaro; MATLAB and Python code with EDF open data, doi:10.5281/zenodo.23562559.

On an island grid, production must match consumption at every moment. The operator needs forecasts of every source and of the total, and they must add up. One model that predicts all of them together can be more coherent, and with an extreme learning machine it trains in seconds.

The question

Corsica's electricity comes from thermal plants, imports through the interconnections, hydropower, solar, wind and bioenergy. The sources are not independent: when solar rises at noon, thermal and imports adjust. Forecasting each source separately ignores these links and gives forecasts whose sum need not equal the forecast total.

Three ways to organize the models

  • SISO: one model per source and per horizon, each reading only the history of its own source.
  • MIMO (the published article): one model per horizon reads the last 48 hours of all sources, plus the hour of the day, and predicts all of them at once.
  • MIMO-MH (the manuscript in submission): a single model predicts all sources for all horizons from 1 to 24 hours, followed by a reconciliation step that makes the forecasts add up.

The theory

Why, in the words of the article: on an island grid the energy sources balance one another (when one varies, the others adjust), so predicting each source and their sum with the same algorithm lets these balancing effects enter the learning, potentially giving a better forecast of the residual demand than one model per source. Six years of hourly data from Corsica (solar, wind, hydropower, thermal, bioenergy and imports, plus the total) are forecast with an extreme learning machine in a multi-input multi-output (MIMO) architecture. Equation numbers are those of the article (arXiv:2508.12764).

Why several outputs: the mutual information (Eqs. 1 and 2)

$$\mathrm{MI}(X;Y)=\iint p(x,y)\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\,p(y)}\,dx\,dy\ \ (1),$$ $$\mathrm{MI}_{\mathrm{norm}}(X;Y)=\frac{\mathrm{MI}(X;Y)}{\sqrt{H(X)\,H(Y)}}\in[0,1]\ \ (2).$$

Unlike the Pearson correlation, it captures non-linear dependencies. Thermal and bioenergy (0.535), solar and bioenergy (0.468), hydropower and thermal (0.373) are strongly linked; the total depends mainly on thermal (0.256), imports (0.231), hydropower (0.129) and solar (0.124). These links support a MIMO approach.

Inputs and outputs

Sliding window
the last \(w\) hours of all seven signals; \(w\) chosen by exhaustive search from 24 to 96 hours with 212 hidden nodes: \(w=48\) is best
Time encoding
\(\sin(2\pi t/24)\) and \(\cos(2\pi t/24)\), with \(t\) the hour of the day
Inputs, outputs
\(7\times48+2\) inputs; 7 outputs, the seven signals at the forecast horizon
Reference
persistence, \(\hat y_{t+h}=y_t\)

The extreme learning machine of the article

For an input vector \(\mathbf x\), hidden neuron \(j\) computes a ReLU of a random projection, and the output is linear in the hidden layer:

$$h_j(\mathbf x)=\max\!\big(0,\ \mathbf w_j^{\top}\mathbf x+b_j\big),$$ $$\mathbf y=H^{\top}W,$$ $$W=H^{+}Y,$$

with \(H^{+}\) the Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse: the input weights and biases are drawn at random and never trained, and the output weights minimize the mean squared error over all outputs in one closed-form step. Fifty random initializations are drawn and the best one, by in-sample RMSE, is kept. With 4,096 hidden neurons the model has 1,413,120 parameters and trains in 104.1 s per initialization on a laptop; the LSTM of the comparison (500 hidden neurons, 1,023,507 parameters) needs 2,651.6 s, about 25 times longer, and an LSTM with 4,096 neurons was infeasible on the same machine. Being closed-form, the ELM can be retrained continuously on a sliding window (online learning).

What the article shows

The ELM significantly outperforms persistence, particularly for solar and thermal production: nRMSE 17.9 % and 5.1 % with R² > 0.98 one hour ahead. Accuracy stays high up to five hours ahead, beyond which renewable sources become increasingly volatile. MIMO gives marginal gains over single-output (SISO) models and, against the LSTM, a closed-form solution at a much lower computational cost, suited to real-time use.

Beyond the article: forecasts that add up (MIMO-MH, manuscript in submission)

The article mentions reconciliation as another strategy for linked quantities. The repository adds, for the multi-horizon extension under submission, a weighted least-squares reconciliation that makes the forecast total equal the sum of the sources: with the summing matrix \(S\) and the error variances \(W\),

$$\tilde{\mathbf y}=S\left(S^{\top}W^{-1}S\right)^{-1}S^{\top}W^{-1}\hat{\mathbf y}.$$

No result of that manuscript is reported here.

Worked example: making forecasts add up

For one hour, suppose the forecasts are 330 MW for the total, 150 for thermal, 90 for imports and 80 for solar, with error variances 36, 25, 49 and 100 MW². The components sum to 320, not 330. Weighted least-squares reconciliation finds the coherent forecasts closest to the original ones, trusting the more reliable forecasts more: thermal 151.2, imports 92.3, solar 84.8 and total 328.3 MW. The gap is closed; solar, the least reliable, moves most. Illustrative values; the formula and its use are in the repository.

Run it

The worked example above, computed with the formula of the theory section (Python 3, NumPy; executed on 22 October 2026). The full pipeline: main in MATLAB for MIMO-ELM, cd mimo_mh && python main.py for SISO, MIMO, MIMO-MH and reconciliation (mimo_mh/reconciliation.py).

import numpy as np

y_hat = np.array([330.0, 150.0, 90.0, 80.0])          # total, thermal, imports, solar (MW)
W = np.diag([36.0, 25.0, 49.0, 100.0])                 # error variances (MW^2)
S = np.vstack([np.ones(3), np.eye(3)])                 # total = sum of the three sources
Wi = np.linalg.inv(W)
y_tilde = S @ np.linalg.solve(S.T @ Wi @ S, S.T @ Wi @ y_hat)
print(y_tilde.round(1))

Output:

[328.3 151.2  92.3  84.8]

Results of the published article

From its abstract: one hour ahead, solar and thermal production are forecast with nRMSE 0.179 and 0.051 and R² above 0.98; total demand stays reliable up to five hours ahead; wind and bioenergy remain hard; MIMO-ELM improves on persistence and on an LSTM tuned with a realistic budget, with small but stable gains over SISO. The MIMO-MH results will be reported with its article.

Code and data

One repository gathers the MATLAB code of the article (MIMO-ELM, SISO and LSTM comparisons), the Python pipeline of MIMO-MH with reconciliation (written by Yoan Jheelan), and the hourly EDF Corse data 2016–2022, released under the Licence Ouverte.

main          % MATLAB: MIMO-ELM with the settings of the article
