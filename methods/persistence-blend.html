BLEND and CLIPER: cyclostationary persistence references for solar forecasting

Principle 3 · Complexity only if it paysThe approach

In the story: climb only if it pays. A persistence reference built on the cyclostationary structure of the signal is the first rung any trained model must beat.

In brief

  • BLEND extends persistence to cyclostationary series: the forecast mixes simple persistence I(t) and cyclic persistence I(t−T+nΔt), with the closed-form weight λ̃ = ½(1 + ρ(t, nΔt)) of the latest value, ρ being the local correlation at that phase.
  • It preserves the periodic variance and covariance, needs no training and no clear-sky model, and on 68 SIAR stations gives accuracy gains over classical persistence, particularly at multi-hour horizons.
  • Published in Applied Mathematical Modelling 157, 116988 (2026) by C. Voyant, C. Banes, L. Garcia-Gutierrez, G. Notton, M. Despotovic and Z. M. Yaseen; audited MATLAB code v1.1.1, doi:10.5281/zenodo.18812333.

Why compare a solar forecast with persistence? BLEND and cyclostationary persistence

What does BLEND add?

The study develops analytical reference forecasts that exploit periodic statistics. The article derives the MSE-optimal weight between simple and cyclic persistence for stationary and periodically stationary processes, and its analytical limit, the closed-form coefficient λ̃ = ½(1 + ρ(t, τ)), where ρ is the local correlation between the current observation and its phase-aligned lag: a training-free baseline that preserves the periodic variance and covariance.

Questions about persistence in solar forecasting

Why compare a solar forecast with persistence?

Persistence costs nothing and is hard to beat at short horizons. A forecast that does not beat a well-defined persistence reference, on the same samples and horizons, adds no demonstrated skill. Report the gain against that reference, for example with the NICEᵏ metrics, and state which persistence you used: simple, clear-sky (“smart”) or cyclostationary.

What is cyclostationary persistence?

Solar irradiance, wind and load do not keep the same statistics through the day: mean, variance and correlation repeat with the cycle. Cyclostationary persistence uses that structure. BLEND weights the latest observation and the same-phase observation of the previous cycle; in its simplest closed form the weight of the latest value is λ̃ = ½(1 + ρ), with ρ the local correlation at that phase (Definition 8 of the article). No network is trained.

The theory

Why, in the words of the article: solar irradiance, wind speed and electrical load have strong diurnal and seasonal periodicities, and their statistics vary with the phase of the cycle, which stationary models and classical persistence cannot capture. The article extends persistence to these cyclostationary processes with closed-form operators that preserve the periodic variance and covariance: a symmetry-induced reduction of the degrees of freedom, and a training-free analytical limit of persistence. Definition and equation numbers are those of the article (arXiv:2602.18949).

Cyclostationarity (Theorem 1)

A process \(I(t)\) of period \(T\) is periodically stationary when its first and second moments repeat with the cycle: \(\mu(t)=\mu(t+T)\), \(\sigma^2(t)=\sigma^2(t+T)\), \(C(t,n\Delta t)=C(t+T,n\Delta t)\), with the phase-dependent correlation

$$\rho(t,n\Delta t)=\frac{C(t,n\Delta t)}{\sigma(t)\,\sigma(t+n\Delta t)} .$$

Time is read on the cyclic group \(\mathbb Z/T\mathbb Z\): \(t\) and \(t-T\) are the same phase.

The persistence operators (Definitions 1 to 5)

  • Simple persistence P (Def. 1): \(\hat I(t+n\Delta t)=I(t)\).
  • Cyclic persistence P⟲ (Def. 2): \(\hat I(t+n\Delta t)=I(t-T+n\Delta t)\), the same phase one cycle earlier.
  • Smart persistence (Def. 3): \(\hat I(t+n\Delta t)=\dfrac{I(t)}{I_{\mathrm{ref}}(t)}\,I_{\mathrm{ref}}(t+n\Delta t)\), with a reference such as clear sky.
  • CLIPER, stationary (Def. 4): \(\lambda\,\mu+(1-\lambda)I(t)\) with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{CLIPER}}=1-\rho(n\Delta t)\); cyclostationary (Def. 5): \(\lambda^{\circlearrowleft}\mu(t)+(1-\lambda^{\circlearrowleft})I(t)\), with \(\lambda^{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathrm{CLIPER}}=\dfrac{[\mu(t+n\Delta t)-\mu(t)]^2+\sigma^2(t)-\rho(t,n\Delta t)\,\sigma(t)\sigma(t+n\Delta t)}{[\mu(t+n\Delta t)-\mu(t)]^2+\sigma^2(t)}\).

BLEND (Definitions 6 to 8)

BLEND is a convex combination of cyclic and simple persistence; \(\lambda\) weights simple persistence:

$$\hat I(t+n\Delta t)=\big(1-\lambda\big)\,I(t-T+n\Delta t)+\lambda\,I(t).$$

Stationary BLEND (Def. 6), MSE-optimal weight from the autocorrelation:

$$\lambda_{\mathrm{BLEND}}=\frac{\rho(T)-\rho(n\Delta t)+\rho(n\Delta t-T)-1}{2\big(\rho(n\Delta t-T)-1\big)} .$$

Periodically stationary BLEND (Def. 7, Eqs. 1 and 2): the weight varies with the phase and carries the periodic means, variances and correlations,

with \(D=(\mu(t)-\mu(t+n\Delta t))^2+\sigma^2(t)+\sigma^2(t+n\Delta t)\), the weight of Eq. (2) reads

$$\lambda^{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathrm{BLEND}}=\frac{(\mu(t)-\mu(t+n\Delta t))^2+\sigma^2(t+n\Delta t)\,[1-\rho(t+n\Delta t,T)]}{D}+\frac{\sigma(t)\sigma(t+n\Delta t)\,[\rho(t,n\Delta t)-\rho(t,n\Delta t-T)]}{D+2\rho(t,n\Delta t-T)\,\sigma(t)\sigma(t+n\Delta t)},$$ $$(2)$$

Simplified BLEND (Def. 8), the analytical limit and the central result of the article:

$$\tilde\lambda^{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathrm{BLEND}}=\tfrac12\big(1+\rho(t,n\Delta t)\big),$$

an optimal shrinkage between instantaneous memory and phase-aligned recurrence (Remark 18): high correlation favors short-term persistence, low or negative correlation the periodic component. It holds under three conditions (Remark 17): equal means and variances at \(t\) and \(t+n\Delta t\); correlation symmetry \(\rho(t+n\Delta t,T)=\rho(t,n\Delta t-T)\); negligible long-lag correlation, \(\rho(t,n\Delta t-T)\approx0\) for \(1\ll n\Delta t\ll T\).

Properties (Appendix D)

With \(\lambda\in[0,1]\) the operator is stable: each forecast lies between \(I(t)\) and \(I(t-T)\) (Theorem 11). It returns a constant signal unchanged. When the denominator of Eq. (2) approaches zero (weak periodic contrast), a mild clipping of \(\lambda\) to \([0,1]\) keeps it stable (Remark 16).

What the article shows

Validation on synthetic cyclostationary series (hybrid bootstrap and Monte Carlo) and on 30-minute irradiation from 68 SIAR stations in Spain (2017–2020), horizons from 30 minutes to 6 hours: embedding the cyclic variance and covariance gives measurable accuracy gains over classical persistence, particularly at multi-hour horizons, even without clear-sky normalization. On the synthetic series, the periodically stationary BLEND and its simplified form are the best models at 1 h and 3 h (Kruskal-Wallis, then pairwise Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon tests); on the measured data, the simplified BLEND offers the most practical balance, accurate across horizons without clear-sky input and at minimal cost (Wilcoxon tests, p < 0.01). AR(p), extreme learning machines or smart persistence can do better under optimized conditions, but they need fitting, prior normalization or learned representations; BLEND reaches comparable robustness at negligible cost, as a reproducible analytical baseline.

Key finding

Accounting for the daily phase improves persistence forecasts on the solar series studied, and the differences widen at longer lead times. In the Applied Mathematical Modelling paper, validation combines controlled cyclostationary simulations with measurements from 68 Spanish stations; on measured data, cyclostationary adjustments reduce errors relative to their naive counterparts, and inter-model differences are tested with Wilcoxon tests. BLEND works directly on raw irradiance, without normalizing by an external clear-sky model.

Results of the open code (v1.1.1, audited)

All BLEND operators share the forecast of the article, Î(t+h) = (1 − λ)·I(t+h−T) + λ·I(t): λ multiplies the latest value. CLIPER mixes the latest value and a mean. The weight is one value for the whole series or one value per phase, and it is estimated on past data. In an out-of-sample benchmark (weights estimated on the first 250 cycles, scored on the next 250, 10 realizations), BLEND with a weight per phase improves on cyclic persistence at every horizon. CLIPER applied to the clear-sky index x/CS has the lowest mean errors, which is consistent with a signal generated as clear sky × correlated noise; stationary BLEND applied to x/CS is much better than BLEND on the raw signal. The simplified weight (1 + ρ)/2 is exact under the assumptions of Remark 17 of the article. These are results of the corrected open code on one synthetic generator (clear sky × correlated noise), different from the article's generators and from its 68 measured stations; they do not replace the article's validation, where the simplified BLEND is among the best at 1 h and 3 h and offers the most practical balance on measured data.

A ladder of reference forecasts

Use references in increasing order of sophistication, and report each one. The first five are documented in Benchmarks for solar radiation time series forecasting (Cyril Voyant, Gilles Notton, Jean-Laurent Duchaud, Luis Antonio Garcia-Gutierrez, Jamie M. Bright, Dazhi Yang, Renewable Energy 191, 747–762, 2022, doi:10.1016/j.renene.2022.04.065) and implemented in ARTU; BLEND comes from the 2026 paper.

Limits

“Training-free” does not mean “observation-free”: cyclic means, deviations and correlations must still be estimated on past data, and they need enough cycles to be stable. BLEND does not outperform every optimized method; autoregressive or learned predictors can do better under some conditions. The evidence concerns the datasets of the paper, not a universal ranking of forecasting methods. Test the transfer to your own series rather than assuming it.
