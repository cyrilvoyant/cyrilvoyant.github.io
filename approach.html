The guiding thread of all the work

Concentric modelling: understand before you compute

People and their question at the centre; a model we understand at the core; every tool around it, AI included. Complexity is added only when it brings a measurable gain.

In brief

  • Cyril Voyant is a physicist and Research Director at Mines Paris – PSL (O.I.E.), after twenty years as a medical physicist in radiotherapy.
  • His work spans solar and energy forecasting, forecast evaluation, radiobiology and language-model interfaces to scientific codes.
  • One discipline runs through it, named here concentric modelling: people at the centre, the model at the core, tools at the periphery.
  • Every page of these sites states which of the five principles below it illustrates.

The five principles

Principle 1

People at the centre

A forecast or a calculation serves a human decision: a physician, a grid operator, an engineer. The question and the responsibility stay human.

In the work: Twenty years of clinical radiotherapy, where every computed dose concerned a patient; forecasts designed for the operators of island grids.

Principle 2

The model at the core

Understand first: physics, signal structure, statistics. Compute directly when the link between inputs and outputs is known; let the machine learn it when it is not.

In the work: Radiobiological dose equivalence computed directly (LQL-Equiv); variability understood before forecasting (sCV and F); signal structure exploited (BLEND).

Principle 3

Complexity only if it pays

Each step — persistence, structured reference, linear model, extreme learning machine, deep network — must beat the previous one under the same protocol. Otherwise, stop.

In the work: Errors measured against persistence (NICE); demanding simple references (BLEND, ARTU); a per-cell ELM beating whole-field deep models with a thousand times fewer parameters (satellite study); frugal benchmarks.

Principle 4

Every tool, at the periphery

Machine learning, deep learning and language models are valuable and used. They remain tools around an understood, validated core: a language model may prepare a calculation, never guess it.

In the work: Multi-output machine learning where the links are unknown (MIMO-ELM), compared with LSTM, U-Net and foundation models; language models that map text to the inputs of validated codes (WebSemantic).

Principle 5

Trust is demonstrated

Evaluate with metrics that match the question; publish code, data and limits; make reuse possible.

In the work: The temporal γ-index, carried over from radiotherapy dose verification; the public audit and correction of BLEND; open code with DOIs and Software Heritage archives; the RTVACOS framework on rigour, transparency, visibility and adoption.

The story

Twenty years in which a model decided a dose

Cyril Voyant is a physicist. From 2003 to 2024 he worked as a medical physicist in radiotherapy, in Montpellier and then in Ajaccio, where he headed the medical physics unit: about ten thousand treatments and the commissioning of a complete radiotherapy platform. In radiotherapy, a wrong model does not cost a point of score; it reaches a patient. Three habits follow: understand the model you use, check it against measurement, and never let the tool decide in place of a person.

From the patient to the power grid

In parallel, his PhD (2011) addressed stochastic modelling and solar forecasting in Corsica, an island whose grid must be balanced at every moment. He carried the clinical habits over. Before forecasting, understand the signal: the course of the sun, the share of the clouds, what can and cannot be predicted. Before adding complexity, beat simple but demanding references.

Measuring what a model adds

Hence evaluation tools that always ask the same question: does a model beat the reference, and at what cost? NICE divides the error by that of persistence; the γ-index, taken from radiotherapy dose verification, judges a forecast within explicit tolerances; frugal benchmarks count parameters next to the error. The same finding keeps returning: a simple, well-posed model is often enough, and more reliable.

In the age of AI: tools around a validated core

Machine learning is used where the link in the data is unknown, as in the multi-source forecasting of the Corsican electricity mix, and compared with deep networks and foundation models. But when the physics is known, it is computed (LQL-Equiv); when data are missing, the system is simulated from what is known (Tunnel Load Simulator); and when a language model is involved, it translates the request into the variables of a validated code, which alone computes (WebSemantic).

Making science findable, credible and reusable

The last link is dissemination: open code and data, DOIs, written limits, pages that state the result and how to cite it. This is the point of the RTVACOS framework and of his op-ed on research excellence: publishing is no longer enough; the work must be found, trusted and reused.

The thread. From the dose to the grid, one discipline: people at the centre, the model at the core, the tools around, and complexity only when it pays.

Short biography

Cyril Voyant is a physicist and Research Director at Mines Paris – PSL (O.I.E.). After twenty years as a medical physicist in radiotherapy, he works on solar and energy forecasting, forecast evaluation and frugal AI. His approach: understand the problem with a model first, add complexity only when it pays, and keep people, not tools, at the centre.
